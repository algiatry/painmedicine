# Laird Hamilton and the Sun: What a Lifetime in the Light Can Teach Us

*Draft article for melanotanii.com. Not for publication on painmedicine.com. See the editor's note at the end before publishing.*

---

Most of us treat the sun as a hazard to be managed. Laird Hamilton treats it as a relationship to be kept.

The big-wave surfer, now in his sixties, has spent more hours under open sky than almost anyone alive. He grew up on the north shores of Oahu and Kauai, helped invent tow-in surfing at Jaws and Teahupoo, and still starts most days in the water. And he has been unusually candid about how he thinks about sunlight. His view is contrarian, occasionally reckless by the standards of dermatology, and worth understanding on its own terms, because underneath the bravado is a coherent philosophy about light, rhythm, and the body.

This article looks at that philosophy honestly: what Hamilton actually does, what the evidence supports, where he and the science part ways, and what it means for anyone who wants a tan without paying for it in skin.

## "If there's no sun, there's no life"

Hamilton's starting point is not skincare. It is biology. In his book *Force of Nature* and in interviews since, he keeps returning to the same idea: the sun is the source of everything, and a body cut off from it gets sick.

That much is not fringe. Light drives the circadian clock. Morning light in particular tells the brain what time it is, anchors sleep, and sets the cortisol rhythm that Hamilton likes to say is "highest in the morning." Chronic under-exposure to daylight is linked in the research to poor sleep, low mood, and vitamin D deficiency. When Hamilton says people get sick from too little sun, he is exaggerating a real signal, not inventing one.

## The sunrise ritual

The most concrete piece of Hamilton's routine is what he calls solar gazing. He wakes before dawn, gets outside, and watches the sun come up over the horizon, taking in the first low-angle light before the day starts. He describes it as grounding, an energy reset, and a way to "set the precedent" for the day.

Strip away the mysticism and there is a defensible practice here. Low-angle sunrise light is rich in the wavelengths that reach the retina's timing cells and light on UV, so it is the safest window of the day for the eyes and the skin. Sleep researchers recommend something very similar: a few minutes of outdoor light soon after waking.

One caution that Hamilton's fans often skip: staring directly at the sun, even at sunrise, can damage the retina. Being outside facing the brightening sky is the useful part. Fixing your gaze on the disc is not.

## The sunscreen argument

This is where Hamilton draws the most fire. He has said plainly that he is "not a big fan of sunscreen" because it blocks his ability to absorb the sun, and that he has never really used it. In one widely shared clip he compared spraying sunscreen on a plant and watching it die.

Here is a fair reading of the claim and the pushback.

**What Hamilton gets right.** Sunlight is not only a carcinogen. It is how the skin makes vitamin D, it influences nitric oxide and blood pressure, and it regulates mood and sleep. A culture that treats every photon as damage has over-corrected.

**What he gets wrong.** The idea that sunscreen meaningfully causes vitamin D deficiency does not hold up. A 2019 review in the *British Journal of Dermatology* found little evidence that real-world sunscreen use lowers vitamin D levels, because people never apply enough to block all UVB. Meanwhile the link between cumulative UV exposure and skin cancer, including melanoma, is among the best-established findings in medicine. Hamilton's own genetics, pigment, and lifelong adaptation are not transferable to a pale office worker on a one-week beach holiday.

**His actual mitigation strategy.** Hamilton is not sun-careless. He argues that much of sun damage comes from skin overheating, and that repeated immersion in cold water keeps the skin cool and limits harm. He builds exposure gradually across a whole year rather than in bursts, and he spends a lot of the day either in water or under a hat. Whatever one thinks of the mechanism, the effect is a body that is rarely burned.

## The pattern worth copying: adaptation, not exposure

If there is a single lesson in Hamilton's relationship with the sun, it is that he never asks his skin to do something it is not prepared for. He is tan in a way that comes from years of graded, daily contact, not from a weekend of forcing it.

That is the distinction that most sun advice misses. Burning is the enemy. A slow, incremental tan is the skin's own protective response: melanin increases, the outer layer thickens, and the skin becomes measurably more resistant to UV. Dermatologists still count a tan as a sign of DNA damage, and they are right that it is. But there is a large difference between the damage of a gradual base and the damage of blistering.

Hamilton's method, in plain terms:

- **Morning light first.** Get outside early, when UV is low, and let the day start there.
- **Short, regular exposure over long, rare exposure.** Daily minutes beat weekend hours.
- **Keep the skin cool.** Water, shade, and breaks matter as much as time in the sun.
- **Never burn.** A red day undoes weeks of adaptation.
- **Respect your own skin type.** His tolerance was built over sixty years in the tropics. Start from yours.

## Where Melanotan II enters the conversation

Readers of this site will know why Hamilton's story is interesting: he represents the natural, slow route to the pigmentation that many people want, and Melanotan II is marketed as a shortcut to it.

It is worth being honest about the difference, because the honest version is the one that keeps readers safe and keeps this site credible.

Melanotan II is a synthetic analogue of the hormone that tells melanocytes to make pigment. It darkens skin with far less UV than a natural tan would need. It is also **not approved by the FDA or any other regulator for any use**, is sold through unregulated channels, and has a case-report literature that includes rapid darkening and eruption of moles, new melanomas, priapism requiring surgery, rhabdomyolysis, kidney infarction, and a reversible brain-swelling syndrome. The FDA has sent warning letters to sellers. No serious clinical trial is currently active.

That does not make every reader's interest illegitimate. It does mean that any article on this topic owes them the full picture: a tanning peptide changes pigment, but it does not provide the thickened skin, the circadian benefit, or the graded adaptation that Hamilton's decades in the light produced. And it adds risks that the sun does not.

If Hamilton's example says anything to someone considering a shortcut, it is this: the tan was never the point. The point was the daily relationship with light, and that part cannot be injected.

## The takeaway

Laird Hamilton is not a model to copy literally. Skipping sunscreen entirely, on his skin, in his life, has worked out for him so far. That is a single case, not a study. But his underlying stance, that the sun is a partner rather than a threat, that morning light matters, that adaptation should be slow and burning should be never, is closer to the emerging science than the blanket fear it pushes back against.

Treat the sun the way he does: early, often, briefly, and with respect. Then make your own decisions about sunscreen with a dermatologist who knows your skin, and about anything stronger with a physician who knows your history.
